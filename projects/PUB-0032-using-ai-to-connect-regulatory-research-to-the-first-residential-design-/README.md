## Overview

This workflow uses AI before, during, and after the initial client meeting for a new house or residential addition.

The first step is regulatory research. Before discussing design options in detail, Codex can help identify the relevant jurisdiction, zoning authority, authority having jurisdiction, applicable code sources, known site constraints, and questions that need confirmation.

The second step is documenting the client’s design program and the initial site conditions. In architectural terms, the design program is the client’s requested set of spaces, uses, relationships, access needs, priorities, and project goals.

The third step is an early comparison between the design program, the initial concept, and the captured regulatory information.

The general sequence is:

`jurisdiction research -> source-backed regulatory brief -> initial site visit -> client design program -> preliminary concept -> regulatory comparison -> professional review`

The purpose is not to produce an automatic approval or final code-compliance determination. The purpose is to place the relevant constraints into the design conversation early enough to influence the initial direction.

## Role

Daniel defines the project question, conducts or directs the client meeting, documents the existing conditions, captures the client’s design priorities, and decides which issues require further review.

Codex assists with:

- locating likely zoning and building-regulation sources;
- organizing the research into a reviewable brief;
- identifying the jurisdiction, AHJ, and code edition when known;
- documenting site observations and client requirements;
- separating facts, requests, assumptions, and open questions;
- comparing the preliminary design with the captured regulatory information; and
- preparing a list of issues for professional or AHJ review.

Codex does not become the building official, zoning official, architect, engineer, attorney, or permit reviewer. Human review remains responsible for deciding whether a source applies and what action should follow.

## Scope Of Work

The workflow can support early feasibility and design intake for:

- new residential construction;
- additions to existing houses;
- accessory dwelling or in-law apartment concepts;
- accessibility-driven residential modifications;
- changes to residential use or occupancy;
- early site and program feasibility discussions; and
- preliminary design coordination.

The research may include:

- zoning classification and permitted uses;
- local land-use requirements;
- applicable building-code sources;
- authority having jurisdiction;
- code edition or effective period;
- known overlays or site restrictions;
- permit-related questions;
- required professional input; and
- unresolved items that cannot be confirmed from the initial research.

The workflow does not replace a survey, engineering analysis, environmental review, legal interpretation, permit submission, or professional design responsibility.

## Key Actions

1. Identify the property jurisdiction and the authority having jurisdiction.

2. Define the initial research questions before the client meeting.

3. Ask Codex to locate official zoning, municipal, state, and building-code sources.

4. Record the source title, link, date, applicable jurisdiction, code edition when known, and relevant language or paraphrase.

5. Separate verified source information from construction interpretation, assumptions, and open questions.

6. Prepare for the initial client meeting with a short regulatory research brief.

7. Document the existing site and building conditions during the visit.

8. Capture the client’s design program, including desired spaces, uses, relationships, access needs, priorities, and concerns.

9. Organize the site observations and client requests into a structured project record.

10. Develop or document an initial design direction.

11. Compare the design direction against the regulatory brief.

12. Identify possible conflicts, missing information, and items requiring confirmation.

13. Route consequential questions to the appropriate professional, zoning official, building official, engineer, or other qualified reviewer.

## Important Features

### Research before programming

Regulatory research takes place before the client’s design requirements are treated as a settled design direction. This gives the conversation a clearer context without prematurely rejecting the client’s goals.

### Program capture

The client’s requests are recorded as a design program rather than scattered meeting notes. The program can include the intended uses, rooms, access requirements, adjacencies, priorities, and unresolved preferences.

### Source traceability

The research brief preserves the path from question to source. A reviewer can see what was searched, which source was used, what date or code edition was identified, and where uncertainty remains.

### Separation of information types

Observed site conditions, client requests, regulatory facts, design assumptions, and unresolved questions are not treated as the same type of information. Keeping those categories separate reduces the chance that an assumption becomes a project fact.

### Early design screening

The initial concept can be reviewed against the regulatory brief before substantial design development occurs. This is a screening step, not a final compliance review.

### Review handoff

The workflow can produce a focused list of issues for a town official, architect, engineer, attorney, or other qualified reviewer instead of presenting an AI-generated conclusion as final.

## Constraints And Decisions

AI can locate and organize sources, but it can also return outdated, incomplete, misapplied, or incorrectly scoped information. A fluent answer is not evidence by itself.

The reviewer must verify:

- the jurisdiction;
- the authority having jurisdiction;
- the applicable code edition or effective period;
- whether the source is official or otherwise appropriate;
- whether local amendments or interpretations apply;
- whether the source addresses the actual project question; and
- whether the issue requires professional or AHJ review.

A design comparison can identify potential conflicts, but it cannot establish approval. A site constraint may require a survey, soil investigation, drainage design, structural analysis, environmental review, or other technical work.

The workflow also avoids treating the client’s initial request as a final program. Client priorities can change as regulatory, site, accessibility, and construction information becomes clearer. The project record should preserve revisions and distinguish confirmed decisions from early ideas.

No client identity, direct address, project-specific regulation, pricing, dimensions, private source files, or permit outcome is included in this public description.

## Outcome

The practical outcome is that regulatory information becomes part of the initial design conversation instead of being treated as a later correction step.

Codex helps reduce the manual effort involved in finding sources, organizing the client’s program, and comparing the first design direction with the available regulatory information. The person responsible for the project still verifies the sources and determines what needs professional or AHJ review.

This approach can reveal potential conflicts earlier, preserve the reasoning behind the initial design direction, and provide a clearer handoff when the project moves from early feasibility into formal design development.
