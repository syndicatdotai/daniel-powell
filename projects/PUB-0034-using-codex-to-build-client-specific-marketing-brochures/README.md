## Overview

This workflow uses Codex to turn recent work records into a focused brochure for a particular client or audience.

The source set can include daily notes, job records, active-project folders, prior deliverables, written project summaries, and attached images. The target client's needs determine what matters. A brochure for a builder may emphasize coordination, handoff, and problem-solving. A brochure for a property owner may emphasize clarity, responsiveness, and the kinds of conditions that can be handled.

The goal is not to publish a complete history of the business. It is to select the smallest useful set of accurate, relevant evidence and turn it into a clear client-facing PDF.

## Role

Daniel defines the audience, the purpose of the brochure, the claims that are acceptable, and the final delivery decision.

Codex assists with:

- finding relevant notes and project records;
- grouping work by type, client need, or outcome;
- locating related images and prior deliverables;
- identifying repeated themes across recent work;
- drafting audience-specific wording;
- connecting each claim to its source; and
- organizing the material into a brochure-ready structure.

Codex does not decide which private information may be shared, grant image permission, invent a project outcome, or replace Daniel's final review.

## Scope Of Work

The workflow can support brochures about:

- construction and repair work;
- field documentation;
- design and drafting support;
- project coordination;
- unusual problem-solving;
- research and material sourcing;
- client communication and handoff; and
- technology-assisted workflows that have a documented connection to the work.

The same source records can support different brochures when the audience changes. The selection and wording should change with the client's actual question instead of repeating a general capabilities list.

## Key Actions

1. Define who will receive the brochure and what decision or conversation it should support.

2. Search the recent daily notes, job records, active-project notes, prior deliverables, and image references.

3. Pull out candidate examples that match the client's needs.

4. Separate documented facts from interpretation, general capability statements, and unresolved details.

5. Verify each meaningful claim against the canonical note or source document.

6. Choose images that explain the work and check whether they are appropriate and cleared for the intended audience.

7. Rewrite the same underlying experience for the recipient's priorities without changing the facts.

8. Assemble a short brochure with a clear opening, relevant examples, supporting images, process language, and a practical next step.

9. Review the PDF for accuracy, privacy, permissions, layout, and unintended identifying details.

10. Send only the reviewed version to the intended recipient.

## Important Features

### Source-backed selection

The useful part is not asking Codex to invent a list of services. It is asking it to find evidence in the records and show where each proposed claim came from.

### Audience-specific framing

The facts may stay the same while the emphasis changes. A builder may care about coordination and clean handoff. A property owner may care about understanding the problem and knowing what happens next. A client interested in technology may care about how field context, images, or structured records improve the process.

### Image context

Images can make a brochure more credible and easier to understand, but they need the same review as the text. The source record should identify what an image shows, which project it came from, whether it contains private information, and whether it can be shared with the intended recipient.

### Reusable brochure structure

Once the source material is organized, Codex can help create a repeatable structure: the client's need, relevant examples, what Daniel did, what the process produced, and what should happen next.

### Human-controlled boundary

Codex widens the search and accelerates the first draft. Daniel remains responsible for deciding whether the source supports the wording, whether the image can be used, and whether the final document is appropriate to send.

## Constraints And Decisions

Recent notes are useful evidence, but they may include shorthand, rough dictation, mixed work, open conditions, or details that were only true at the time of the entry. A note is a starting point for verification, not automatic marketing copy.

The workflow must also prevent accidental disclosure. A client-specific brochure should exclude private identities, direct addresses, contact information, internal identifiers, pricing, measurements, sensitive documents, and any combination of facts that would make a project easy to identify.

Images may contain faces, addresses, documents, license plates, private messages, metadata, or other information that was not intended for external distribution. Image selection therefore needs a separate permission and privacy check.

Tailoring the wording does not mean overstating the work. The brochure should distinguish completed work, current capability, proposed workflow, and future possibility.

## Deliverables

The workflow can produce:

- a client-specific evidence shortlist;
- a source-to-claim ledger;
- a reviewed set of image candidates;
- audience-specific brochure wording;
- a structured PDF draft;
- a privacy and permission checklist; and
- a final brochure ready for human delivery review.

The exact format can change with the recipient. The source trail and review boundary should remain consistent.

## Outcome

The practical outcome is a brochure that explains relevance instead of merely listing experience.

It can connect a client's current concern to real examples from recent work, show the process in language that makes sense to that recipient, and use images that help the reader understand the work without exposing private project details.

The value comes from combining retrieval, comparison, writing, and document preparation in one controlled workflow. Codex helps find the material. The job notes provide the evidence. Daniel decides what is accurate, appropriate, and ready to send.

## Tools And Methods

- **Codex:** source search, context retrieval, grouping, comparison, drafting, and document preparation;
- **Obsidian Markdown records:** daily notes, job history, project notes, source-of-truth documents, and supporting evidence;
- **Image and deliverable folders:** candidate images, prior PDFs, layouts, and related project artifacts;
- **Evidence ledger:** mapping meaningful public claims to the notes that support them;
- **Audience filter:** selecting and rewriting only what serves the intended client conversation; and
- **Human review:** privacy, accuracy, image permission, layout, and final delivery approval.

## Evidence And Limitations

This record is grounded in Daniel's current request and the private field-capture brochure project, which documents a PDF-oriented process with source content, image candidates, drafts, exports, and review artifacts. Recent daily and job notes provide examples of the kind of work that can be searched for relevant context.

The public record describes a practical workflow pattern, not a claim that one unattended command automatically understands every job, selects every image correctly, or produces a send-ready brochure without review. Source notes can be incomplete or ambiguous, and image permission depends on the specific asset and recipient.

No client names, job identifiers, direct addresses, private filenames, pricing, measurements, or private images are included. Any future client-facing brochure would need its own source check, privacy review, image-permission review, and final approval before delivery.
