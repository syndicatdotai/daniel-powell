# Keeping Job Conditions and Notes in Obsidian

## LinkedIn Summary

I keep a Markdown file in Obsidian for each job. I dictate my field notes into Codex, and it helps organize the information under the right job, along with photos, drawings, and related correspondence.

I record the conditions that affect the work: something that's out of square, an access problem, damaged material, or a detail that needs to match what's already there. I also record decisions made on site and why we made them.

As the job progresses, I add what was completed, what still needs attention, and anything that changes the next visit. That might be materials I need to pick up, equipment I've left there, or work that has to wait until something is dry.

Measurements stay with the explanation of where they came from. Photos stay with the notes about what they show. If something hasn't been confirmed, I leave that clear in the record.

Before heading back, I can have Codex pull together the current notes and what I need for that visit.

## Overview

I use Obsidian as an ongoing record of my construction jobs. Each job has its own Markdown file, with related photos, drawings, and supporting documents stored alongside it.

The notes develop as the work progresses. An initial scope might come from an email or a phone call. A site visit adds observations and measurements. Later entries record progress, decisions, and changes that affect the remaining work.

I use voice dictation with Codex to capture that information and help organize it into the appropriate job record.

## Role

I carry out the site observations, supply the measurements, and describe the work and decisions. Codex helps structure those updates, connect them to the existing job, and retrieve the relevant information later.

The job record brings together my field notes and supporting material from the people involved in the work.

## Scope Of Work

The records cover initial intake, existing conditions, site visits, work progress, and follow-up requirements. They also connect to drawings, correspondence, tasks, schedules, and equipment records where applicable.

Some jobs need only a short entry and a few photos. Others accumulate detailed field notes and several phases of work. I keep the information appropriate to the job.

## Key Actions

### Record conditions that affect the work

I capture details that will matter when planning or carrying out the next step. That includes irregular surfaces, restricted access, damaged materials, obstructions, and the relationship between new work and existing features.

For example, a railing measurement needs the context of the surrounding stairs and landings. A note about an irregular corner or sloping surface belongs with the measurements so it can be considered when preparing the drawing.

### Keep the reasoning with the decision

When a condition changes the installation sequence, I record why. If a surface needs to be finished before later work closes off access, that explanation stays in the job history.

The same applies to selections and scope changes. A requested detail, a confirmed selection, and an item still awaiting a decision need to remain distinguishable.

### Update progress in useful detail

I record progress by area or phase when that helps describe the actual state of the work. One area may be ready for finishing while another still needs preparation.

The update can also include what I need for the next visit: materials to collect, tools already on site, or a condition that determines whether the next operation can proceed.

## Important Features

### Measurements retain their context

Raw field readings remain connected to their reference points and descriptions. An existing feature measured for reference is identified separately from a requirement for new work.

When calculations or further confirmation are still needed, that remains visible. I can return to the original observation without having to reconstruct how a number was obtained.

### Photos and drawings remain connected to the notes

Photos are stored with the job and linked from its record. The accompanying commentary explains the condition or stage of work they document.

Drawing files and later exports can also be linked from the same record, along with notes about their preparation or delivery.

### Current information sits alongside dated history

The job file can have a quick reference section for the scope, important conditions, and current requirements, followed by dated entries.

That history preserves how the job developed. A later confirmation can update an earlier assumption while leaving the original sequence understandable.

## Constraints And Decisions

I keep missing or conflicting information visible. A tentative appointment is different from a completed visit, and finishing one phase does not necessarily mean the whole job is finished.

I also keep scope boundaries in the notes. Existing features that are only references, work assigned to someone else, and possible additions need to be identifiable when reviewing the job.

## Deliverables

The result is a job record containing the applicable field observations, measurements, decisions, progress notes, and links to supporting files.

Tasks and schedule entries connect the record to upcoming work. Equipment links help identify what has been left at the job and what may need to be collected.

## Outcome

Before returning to a job, I can review its latest entries or ask Codex to pull together the relevant context. That gives me the recorded conditions, outstanding work, and preparation for the visit in one place.

As more information comes in, I continue adding it to the same job history.

## Tools And Methods

The workflow uses Obsidian, Markdown files, Codex, voice dictation, linked photos and drawings, dated job entries, and connected task, schedule, and equipment records.

## Evidence And Limitations

This article draws on a review of recent job records, with identifying details removed. The records vary in depth, and summary fields sometimes need reconciliation with newer dated entries.
