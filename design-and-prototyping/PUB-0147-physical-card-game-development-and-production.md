# Developing a Physical Card Game Through Iteration, Playtesting, and Production

Beginning in 2014, I developed a physical strategy game as a personal project outside my regular work. It went through several years of experimentation, rule changes, visual development, and playtesting before reaching a small manufactured prototype batch. A printable version was documented in 2019, with further card-layout and Adobe Illustrator revisions continuing into 2020.

This work preceded my use of AI. The process involved developing the mechanics, testing them with people, revising the design, preparing production files, and arranging overseas printing.

The project began with a much larger scope. Through playtesting, it became a compact two-player card game built around simultaneous decisions, asymmetric matchups, and the consequences of using a limited set of cards.

## Starting With a Simple Interaction

The original inspiration was rock–paper–scissors.

Its appeal was the relationship between a very small set of choices and the uncertainty created by another person making a decision at the same time. Learning about competitive rock–paper–scissors drew my attention to the psychology of repeated play: anticipating an opponent, recognizing sequences, and considering whether someone would repeat a choice or change direction.

I wanted to explore that interaction through a physical card game, introducing asymmetry into the available choices.

The cards would have different matchup relationships. Their usefulness would depend on what the opponent selected and when each card was played.

That became the central design problem: introduce meaningful differences between the choices while preserving the directness that made the original interaction interesting.

## Simultaneous Selection and a Limited Hand

The simplified game used **simultaneous action selection**. Both players chose a card privately and revealed their selections together. A battle diagram determined the outcome of the pairing.

Each player received seven unique cards, and each card could be played only once during the match. After all cards had been played, the player with the most victories won.

The single-use rule changed the decisions from round to round.

Playing a card committed that option. It also revealed information to the opponent and changed the choices remaining for subsequent rounds. Players had to consider the immediate matchup alongside the value of retaining a particular card for later.

The intended strategy came from that combination: unequal matchup relationships, simultaneous decisions, and a shrinking set of available options. Tracking previously played cards and anticipating the opponent’s next selection became part of the game.

## Using Playtesting to Reduce the Scope

The early design grew into a much larger board game with additional systems, more rules, and sessions that took hours to complete.

Playtesting exposed the problem. There was too much for players to learn and manage, and the game took too long. Features that had seemed interesting during development added complexity without strengthening the central interaction.

The design changed rapidly once people began playing it.

Over successive revisions, most of the surrounding game was removed. The combat-card system became the focus, and the project developed into a two-player experience.

This was the hardest part of the work. Considerable effort had already gone into mechanics that ultimately needed to be cut. Removing them meant setting aside work that I valued because the playtesting showed the game needed a simpler structure.

Those decisions shaped the finished prototype. The project became more focused as the rules concentrated on the interaction players were actually engaging with.

## Developing the Cards in Adobe Illustrator

Adobe Illustrator was the main tool used to develop the card layouts and prepare the production artwork.

The surviving files show multiple generations of card designs, revised layouts, and supporting materials. The physical set included the play cards, card backs, instructions, and reference information explaining how the matchups were resolved.

The visual work had to support the rules. Players needed to recognize their options and understand the relationship between the cards during play. As the mechanics changed, the documents and artwork also needed revision.

Preparing the game for printing brought those pieces together into a production-ready package. The rules, card identities, reference materials, and visual layouts had to describe the same version of the game.

A supporting website also formed part of the project.

## Producing and Distributing a Prototype Batch

Once the design and files were ready, I arranged a small prototype print run through an overseas manufacturer and had the finished cards delivered.

That moved the project beyond digital artwork and locally prepared test materials. It produced physical copies that could be distributed to other people for evaluation.

Copies were sent to playtesters in different parts of the United States. The intention was to gather feedback from people outside the immediate development process and see how the simplified game performed with a broader group.

The response was limited and did not generate the level of participation or feedback I had hoped for. The project was subsequently put on hold.

## A Personal Project Taken Through Physical Production

The work extended across several years of spare-time development. It included concept development, rules design, playtesting, scope reduction, graphic design, production preparation, manufacturer coordination, and distribution.

The most demanding decisions involved simplifying the product. Testing repeatedly showed that the strongest version required removing substantial portions of the original design.

By the time the project was put on hold, it had progressed from an expansive board-game concept to a manufactured two-player card-game prototype. It remains an example of my earlier approach to developing and testing ideas: building something tangible, observing how people use it, and revising the design around that experience.
