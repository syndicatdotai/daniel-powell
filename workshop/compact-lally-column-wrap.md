# Wrapping a Lally Column Without Building a Wall Around It

An exposed support column creates a finishing problem out of proportion to its size. The column itself occupies little floor space, but conventional framing can turn it into a much larger architectural element.

Daniel developed a compact wrapping method that used folded drywall, low-pressure expanding foam, and metal corner bead to create an approximately five-inch-square enclosure around a four-inch-diameter column. The approach eliminated the separate stud framework and produced a firmly attached finish with a substantially smaller footprint.

## The Problem With Conventional Boxing

Lally columns support beams carrying loads from the structure above. Four-inch-diameter columns are a common configuration, although other sizes are available. [Column manufacturer information](https://www.portlandstoneware.com/products/building-products/structural-lock-system/lally-lock-column/)

That diameter creates an immediate coordination issue. A conventional two-by-four wall has a framing depth of three and a half inches. A common steel-stud partition uses three-and-five-eighths-inch studs. A four-inch column extends beyond either framing depth, requiring an adjustment to the wall assembly or a separate enclosure.

The visual problem becomes more pronounced when the column stands in the middle of a finished room. Customers often want it boxed neatly while preserving as much space as possible.

In one straightforward framing arrangement, placing a stud on either side of a four-inch column adds three inches across that dimension. Half-inch drywall on the two outside faces brings the enclosure to approximately eight inches wide. Across the other direction, the column and drywall occupy approximately five inches.

The result is an eight-by-five-inch rectangle before allowing for corner bead and finishing compound. Making that enclosure square increases it to approximately eight by eight inches. Thinner backing materials can reduce the size, but the supporting framework still adds to the finished envelope.

Daniel approached the problem by reconsidering what the enclosure actually needed to do.

## Using the Drywall as the Enclosure

The building’s load was already carried by the column. The surrounding assembly needed to provide a straight, durable, finished surface.

Instead of constructing a separate framework and attaching drywall to it, Daniel formed the drywall itself into a continuous folded enclosure.

He laid a full-height piece flat with the face upward, marked the longitudinal sections, and scored through one face. Breaking the gypsum along those lines while preserving the backing paper created connected panels that could fold around the column.

The retained paper held the sections together during assembly. Three corners remained connected through those folds, leaving one open seam where the two ends met.

This produced the basic square enclosure without adding studs around the steel.

## Connecting the Wrap to the Column

Before closing the wrap, Daniel applied Great Stuff window-and-door foam to the inward-facing surface of the drywall.

He selected that formulation for its low expansion pressure. As the drywall closed around the column, the foam contacted the steel and bridged the spaces between the round column and the square enclosure.

The foam provided distributed contact behind the drywall. Once cured, those connections held the enclosure against the column and supported the finished faces.

Although the window-and-door foam itself remains flexible, Daniel found that the completed combination of steel, foam, folded drywall, and corner bead felt solid under a firm knock.

## Closing the Seam With Corner Bead

The remaining challenge was holding the open seam together while the foam cured.

Daniel prepared the metal corner beads before wrapping the column. After bringing the drywall ends together, he installed a corner bead over the closing corner and secured its flanges to the adjoining faces with a crown stapler.

The bead served two purposes: it established the finished corner and held the enclosure closed during curing.

He then installed the remaining corner beads while the assembly was still adjustable. Together, the beads established straight edges around the enclosure and helped maintain its square shape.

## Aligning the Finished Enclosure

The uncured foam left time to adjust the wrap’s orientation. Daniel could rotate and square the enclosure to the room rather than allowing the position of the round steel column to dictate the appearance of the finished faces.

He also held the drywall slightly above the floor with temporary shims. That separation kept its bottom edge from resting directly against the floor and reduced the opportunity for moisture to wick into the gypsum.

Once the enclosure was aligned, the foam cured in place. On his installations, it developed enough hold for finishing work to follow later in the same work session. The timing depended on the application and site conditions.

## Finishing the Surface

With the wrap secured, Daniel finished the corners and surfaces using setting-type joint compound, such as Easy Sand 20 or Durabond.

The corner beads provided the straight edges needed to finish the enclosure as a small architectural column. The folded drywall supplied the faces, and the foam connected those faces to the steel support within.

Around a four-inch column, half-inch drywall brought the basic enclosure to approximately five inches square, with corner bead and compound adding a small finishing allowance.

## The Result

The method reduced the enclosure from a conventional framed box to a compact finish closely fitted around the existing column.

Its value came from assigning several jobs to the same materials. The drywall formed the enclosure. The retained paper connected the folded panels. The corner bead established the edges and held the closing seam. The foam connected the wrap to the column behind it.

Daniel developed this approach to meet a recurring customer preference: conceal the exposed support without turning it into an oversized feature in the room. The result preserved a square appearance, minimized the footprint, and produced the solid feel he wanted from the finished assembly.
