# Stepping Into Greater Responsibility on the Watermark Projects

One of the formative periods in my construction career came while working on commercial remodeling projects at the Watermark facilities in Connecticut: The Watermark at 3030 Park in Bridgeport and The Watermark at East Hill in Southbury.

I was working as a subcontractor for a company handling the Division 9 scope. My responsibility was on the steel-stud framing crew. I was still a younger carpenter, but I already had practical field experience and the ability to read blueprints that I had developed through my work at Vinyl Tech.

## Taking on the communication

During the framing work in Bridgeport, I saw an opportunity to improve communication between our framing crew and the general contractor’s leadership. I could follow the drawings, understand the assembly methods, and discuss practical options when an issue came up in the field.

I began stepping into those conversations. I could speak with confidence about how we were assembling the work, what the schedule required, and what options we had for resolving an on-site problem.

Our internal leadership recognized that I could help improve the communication and allowed me to take more of the lead in speaking with the appropriate stakeholders. It was an informal increase in responsibility rather than a change in my title.

I remained part of the framing crew while increasingly representing the company in discussions with the general contractor. That gave me an early introduction to communicating with people at higher levels in the organizations we worked for.

## Laying out walls with a changing radius

The Watermark work was also my first real experience laying out progressive-radius walls, where the radius changes along the face of the wall.

The curves were large enough that the radial center points shown on the architectural drawings fell deep inside other parts of the building, outside our construction area. We could not establish a direct line of sight from those points to the wall. As the curvature changed along the wall, there was also no single fixed center from which to lay out the whole shape.

The drawings showed the intended geometry and radial center points, but they did not give us the intermediate field measurements needed to lay out the wall from within the work area.

I used a scaled grid on the blueprint to transfer that geometry into the field. I drew the grid over the plan, took measurements from the drawing, and transferred the corresponding points to the actual job conditions.

Those points gave us a way to establish the wall’s path without physically reaching the distant center points. I traced the curve in segments between the transferred points, then walked up and down the length of the wall, checking the changing curve and making small adjustments so the segments flowed together.

The work brought blueprint reading and practical layout together. I could understand the intended shape on the plan, but I also had to find a way to reproduce it within the access and visibility constraints of the building.

## Fiberglass architectural features

These projects also gave me my first experience installing decorative radial fiberglass columns and suspended inverted fiberglass domes.

Those installations expanded the specialty-interior work I had performed beyond steel-stud framing and drywall. They added experience with formed architectural components and suspended decorative features.

## Growing into a representative role

My ability to read the prints mattered because I could participate directly in technical discussions. My hands-on experience mattered because I understood what the decisions meant for the people building the work.

The progressive-radius layout was a concrete example of that combination: interpreting the drawings, recognizing why a conventional center-point layout would not work in the available space, and using a practical method to establish the intended curve.

That responsibility carried through the projects we worked on for the management company at the Watermark facilities in Connecticut. It became one of my early experiences of earning trust beyond my immediate installation work and being accepted as someone who could represent the company in conversations about assemblies, scheduling, and field solutions.
